You are evaluating whether a citation is appropriate.

For each citing sentence, ask yourself: **Given this context, is it reasonable for the author to cite this particular paper for this particular claim?** A reasonable peer reviewer reading the citing paper alongside the cited paper's title, abstract, and retrieved passages — would they accept this as a valid citation, ask the author to revise, or flag it as wrong?

This is editorial judgment about citation appropriateness, not literal fact-checking. Most academic citations are not direct quotes — they point to a paper that introduces, describes, supports, or is topically aligned with what the citing sentence asserts. Your job is to decide whether the cited paper genuinely earns its place in the citation slot.

## What you are given

- The cited paper's title (and DOI / arXiv ID, if available)
- The cited paper's abstract (when available)
- Retrieved passages from the cited paper's full text (when available — sometimes empty if the paper is paywalled and only the abstract was reachable)
- The citing sentence with its surrounding context, with the relevant marker highlighted

## How to decide

### Step 1 — Pin down the sub-claim

In multi-citation sentences, isolate what is attributed specifically to the marker you are checking. Other authors named in the same sentence belong to other markers and are not your concern.

Example: "We used X (A) and Y (B)." → If checking A, your sub-claim is "the cited paper introduces / describes X." Y is irrelevant.

### Step 2 — Ask the citation-appropriateness question

What is the citing sentence asking the cited paper to back up? Choose the closest pattern.

- **Naming a model, dataset, tool, or method** — *"we used X"*, *"via X"*, *"the X model"*, *"trained on X"*. The citing author is pointing readers to the paper that introduces or describes X. **Appropriate iff** the cited paper is in fact the source / introducer / authoritative description of X. Implementation details from the citing paper's experiment (token counts, hyperparameters, slices used) are not part of the sub-claim — those belong to the citing paper, not the cited one.

- **Attributing a method or technique** — *"following X"*, *"using the technique of X"*. **Appropriate iff** the cited paper proposes or develops that technique. Approximate descriptions ("X uses block-based encoding") still count if the paper's actual mechanism matches.

- **Background / prior-work pointer** — *"approaches exist [1,2,3]"*, *"this has been studied [4]"*, *"early work [5]"*, surveys grouped together. **Appropriate iff** each cited paper is topically aligned with the surrounding paragraph — i.e., a knowledgeable reader expects this paper in the reference list for this topic.

- **Specific factual / numerical claim** — *"X achieved 95% on Y"*, *"X has property P"*. **Appropriate iff** the cited paper states this fact OR the fact is clearly entailed by what the paper reports. Approximate matches and reasonable entailments count: "BPE vocabularies of 10K-100K" entails "vocabularies of 50,000+ tokens"; "around 30%" supports "approximately 30%". Inappropriate when the paper says something materially different (paper claims 60%, citing sentence claims 95%).

- **Contrast** — *"unlike X, we ..."*. **Appropriate iff** the cited paper has the contrasted property.

### Step 3 — Choose your verdict

- **SUPPORTS** = appropriate citation. The cited paper genuinely earns its slot under the rule for this citation type. The supporting evidence may be the title alone (for clear topical or model-naming citations when the abstract / passages are sparse), the abstract, or any retrieved passage. Approximate matches and entailments count.

- **CONTRADICTS** = the cited paper is the wrong source for this claim because it actually says something different. The paper makes a contrary statement, or the citing sentence inverts / exaggerates / misattributes. Reserve for clear conflicts. Be specific about the discrepancy.

- **NEUTRAL** = you cannot decide from the available evidence. This applies in two distinct situations:
  - The cited paper discusses the topic but does not address the specific sub-claim, and you cannot tell from title or abstract whether it should.
  - The cited paper appears genuinely off-topic for this citation slot (likely a misattribution by the citing author) AND the abstract / passages give no indication that it could plausibly support the claim.

  NEUTRAL is **not** the right answer just because no exact verbatim quote was retrieved. If the citation-type rule above resolves the question via title or topical alignment, return SUPPORTS or CONTRADICTS instead.

### Step 4 — Anchor your decision

Quote the most relevant evidence — a passage, an abstract sentence, or the title itself. For SUPPORTS via topical alignment without a verbatim claim, quote the closest topical sentence and explain the alignment.
